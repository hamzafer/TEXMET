An Exploration of Deep Learning Methods for Textile Reconstruction

Muhammad Hamza Zafar
Supervisors: Prof. Jon Yngve Hardeberg and Dr. Davit Gigilashvili
NTNU - Norwegian University of Science and Technology

Abstract

The reconstruction of fragmented historical textiles presents a significant challenge for archaeologists. The process is often manual, slow, and prone to bias. While AI-powered puzzle solvers have shown promise in other domains, they often fail when applied to archaeological data due to a "domain gap." These models, typically trained on natural images, struggle with the irregular shapes, degradation, and missing information characteristic of ancient textiles.

To address this challenge, we introduce TEXMET, a large-scale, curated dataset of textile and tapestry images from the Metropolitan Museum of Art. TEXMET is the first benchmark dataset specifically designed for textile reconstruction, containing over 18,000 high-resolution images. The dataset was created through an automated pipeline of downloading, filtering, and curating images from the MET's open-access archive.

Our research investigates the performance of state-of-the-art puzzle-solving models on textile data, the potential for domain-specific fine-tuning with TEXMET to improve performance, the impact of various preprocessing techniques, and the capabilities of Large Vision Models (LVMs) in this context.

Models & Architectures

We evaluated several state-of-the-art models for puzzle solving and image reconstruction:

  • JPDVT: A Diffusion-based Vision Transformer model, known for its robustness to missing patches.
  • FCViT: A Transformer-based model that uses coordinate regression.
  • DiffAssemble: A graph-based diffusion model that reasons about piece-to-piece relationships.

We also explored the use of other models and techniques, including:

  • ASUKA: An inpainting model to reconstruct missing parts of the images.
  • GPT-4o and Gemini: Large Vision-Language Models (LVMs) to explore their capabilities for tasks like tile arrangement, narrative generation, and semantic retrieval.

Experiments

Our research involved a series of experiments to evaluate the performance of the selected models and the impact of our TEXMET dataset.

  • Model Reproduction and Baseline: We first reproduced the results of the chosen models and established a baseline performance on the ImageNet dataset.
  • Oseberg Benchmarking: When applied to real-world Viking textile fragments from the Oseberg collection, all models showed a significant drop in performance, highlighting the domain gap.
  • Masking and Inpainting: We tested the models' resilience to missing information by applying masks and using inpainting techniques.
  • TEXMET Fine-tuning: This was a key experiment where we fine-tuned the models on our TEXMET dataset. The results were remarkable, especially for the DiffAssemble model, which showed a significant improvement in performance on both TEXMET and the Oseberg dataset.
  • Cropping Strategy: We used a cropping strategy to remove borders and force the models to learn the textile patterns themselves, rather than relying on edge information.

Our experiments demonstrated that domain-specific fine-tuning with a high-quality dataset like TEXMET is crucial for achieving good performance in textile reconstruction.

Conclusion

Our work makes several key contributions to the field of computational archaeology and computer vision:

  • We introduced TEXMET, the first large-scale benchmark dataset for textile reconstruction.
  • We demonstrated that the DiffAssemble model, when fine-tuned on TEXMET, achieves state-of-the-art performance in reconstructing Viking textiles.
  • We showed that it is crucial for models to learn the underlying patterns of the textiles, not just rely on shortcuts like edge information.
  • We explored the capabilities of Large Vision Models (LVMs) and found them to be useful as assistive tools for tasks like metadata generation and semantic retrieval, rather than for solving the reconstruction puzzle itself.

Our findings pave the way for future research in developing specialized architectures and hybrid pipelines that combine the strengths of different models to tackle the challenges of historical textile reconstruction.
