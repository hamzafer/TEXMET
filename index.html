Overview

Teaching puzzle solvers about textiles

Reconstructing fragmented historical textiles is slow, manual work for archaeologists. AI puzzle solvers look promising, but they are trained on natural photographs and struggle with the faded colors, worn fibers and missing parts of ancient fabric: a classic domain gap.

To close it we introduce TEXMET, the first benchmark dataset designed for textile reconstruction: 18,644 high-resolution textile and tapestry images from the Metropolitan Museum of Art's Open Access collection, curated with CLIP embeddings, UMAP clustering, similarity search and human review.

We study how state-of-the-art puzzle solvers perform on textiles, how much domain-specific fine-tuning on TEXMET helps, how missing content and inpainting affect them, and what Large Vision Models (LVMs) can contribute.

The problem

The Oseberg find

The Oseberg ship burial (834 CE, Norway) held some of the finest textiles of the Viking Age. Today they survive as hundreds of fragile, degraded fragments at the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo, and nobody knows exactly how they originally fit together.

The dataset

Building TEXMET

No existing dataset fit: textile collections show modern fashion, fragment datasets show frescoes, and puzzle benchmarks use everyday photos. So we built one from The Met's open collection.

The solvers

Models & architectures

Three state-of-the-art puzzle solvers, all trained on ImageNet, reproduced and evaluated on textiles.

CVPR 2024

JPDVT

A diffusion vision transformer: starts from a noisy guess of where pieces go and refines it step by step. Robust to missing patches.

2025

FCViT

A vision transformer that predicts each piece's position directly by coordinate regression.

CVPR 2024

DiffAssemble

A graph-based diffusion model that reasons about piece-to-piece relationships; it can handle rotation, missing pieces and variable puzzle sizes.

ASUKA inpainting

Fills in missing regions of damaged images before puzzle solving.

GPT-4o and Gemini

Large Vision Models explored for tile arrangement, narrative generation and semantic retrieval.

Results

Fine-tuning on TEXMET closes the gap

Puzzle accuracy: the share of 3×3 puzzles with every piece in the right place. Trained on ImageNet only, the solvers struggle on textiles and on real Oseberg fragments. Fine-tuned on TEXMET, DiffAssemble more than doubles its Oseberg score. Oseberg is never used for training.

ModelImageNetTEXMETOseberg (45 fragments)
JPDVT (ImageNet-trained)67.9%32.9%20.0%
FCViT (ImageNet-trained)68.8%8.3%24.4%
DiffAssemble (ImageNet-trained)56.6%65.1%26.7%
DiffAssemble fine-tuned on TEXMET–85.9% +20.864.4% +37.8

Textile features, not just outlines

On square crops from inside each Oseberg fragment (no outline), the fine-tuned model still beats the baseline: 39.8% vs 27.7%.

Robust to holes

On ImageNet photos with a third hidden by a real fragment-shaped mask, DiffAssemble barely moves while FCViT drops 24.5 points. Inpainting recovers part of the loss.

Fine-tuning isn't automatic

JPDVT forgot what it knew (catastrophic forgetting) and FCViT barely adapted; DiffAssemble was carried forward.

Takeaways

Conclusions and lessons

Conclusion
  • We introduced TEXMET, the first large-scale benchmark dataset for textile reconstruction.
  • DiffAssemble fine-tuned on TEXMET gives the best results on real Viking textile fragments (26.7% → 64.4% on Oseberg).
  • Models must learn the textile patterns themselves, not shortcuts like outlines: our crop experiments test exactly that.
  • Large Vision Models are useful as assistive tools (metadata, semantic retrieval), not for solving the puzzle itself.
Future work

Move beyond grid puzzles to irregularly shaped fragments, for example with graph representations that encode boundary shapes or attention focused on contour matching. Build larger, more diverse archaeological datasets for pre-training. Explore hybrids where an LVM's semantic understanding guides the spatial search of a solver like DiffAssemble.

Reproduction challenges

Reproducing published models took far longer than expected: the 4–6 weeks per model initially planned became 8–12 weeks for problematic models like FCViT. That bottleneck shaped the research, which is why we share code and data to help others avoid the same pitfalls.

Compute

Most experiments ran on NTNU's IDUN HPC cluster on H100 and A100 nodes (4 GPUs, 32 CPUs, 200 GB RAM per node); DiffAssemble needed A100 nodes for older PyTorch compatibility. About 3,000 GPU hours in total, plus smaller tests on a local RTX 4090. Slurm scripts are in hpc-jobs-ntnu. We thank IDUN NTNU HPC for the resources.
